I've had an interesting experience the past few days trying to get information about my insurance policies. It started when I wanted confirmation that my auto policy will cover me when I rent a car as I believed it had for the past 30 years. I just wanted to be sure. So I called my agent. Three times. And never got a straight answer. I then called a friend who is an agent at a different insurance company about switching insurance. She gave me a straight answer in one minute.

I had a different question about coverage under my home-owners policy. At this point,
I was loath to call my agent and I was not sure I would believe any answer I got.
The former owner had retired recently. She would stay on the phone with you till
9 at night to answer your questions. I called again. I got a person giving answers
like "I can't answer that, I'll have to transfer you to an agent". Hold for 10 minutes.
"Nobody is available, can they call you back" (me, reluctantly) "OK". Nobody calls
me back. So I call several hours later. They've just talked to my wife about something
else in the meantime, but hadn't returned my call.

The plan from here was to switch to a different agency with the same insurer. Just
transfer the policy. Call them in a sales situation and see if a) they answer the
phone b) they will answer my question about coverage. But 3 calls later, I never
got past "leave a number".

OK. Plan C. Feed my policy to ChatGPT and get the answer straight from the horses
mouth. After working through downloading the policy, discovering that they encoded
the PDF so you can't easily get text out of it, researching PDF to text OCR solutions,
getting one to work on my laptop and FINALLY uploading the text of my policy to ChatGPT
I had my answer in about 30 seconds. Straight from the text of my policy. Done.

I then fed the full text of both my old policy and potential new policy to ChatGPT
and got a very understandable side-by-side comparison of the coverages. Very nice.

What does this mean?

It means I'm not going to look for answers from people at my old agency who clearly don't have the same motivation and knowledge the old owner did. It means I'm not going to switch to a different agency that won't even answer the phone for a sales call. It means I'm going to switch to the company where my friend is an agent because a) they answer the phone b) seem to know what they're talking about c) gave me a lower rate.

What does it mean in term of AI?

It means I can get answers without talking to people who may not know what they are
talking about (or who just won't give me the time of day). It means I can get my
own answers quickly from the authoritative source using AI directly.

What does it mean for people's jobs?

It means we don't have to take Tesla's to the blacksmith to get new horseshoes. We
don't have a lot of blacksmiths today. We don't need them. People are doing more
productive things with their time. I suppose we could have government regulations
and subsidies for blacksmiths and unions protecting their jobs. People don't have
to work in dead-end call center jobs. People don't have to work as insurance agents
dispensing questionably accurate information about policies they only half understand.

When the camera was invented, I'm sure there was push-back form portrait painters, but painters moved on to things the camera could not do: impressionist and abstract art, etc. I'm glad we got Monet, Picasso, etc.  When the printing press was invented we got mass literacy because we were no longer dependent on sciptoria full of monks hand-copying every manuscript.  There will be disruption, but the world  and people's lives will be better in the end.

Your assignment is to create yourself an account, log in, run Emacs, create a file,
and save it. Bonus points for running dired. Double bonus points if it's a finger
plan file (the TOPS20 version of "About Me"). Can you edit a file in Emacs c.a. 1980?
How much of what you do today in Emacs was there at the beginning? The past is a
foreign land.

Emacs was created on multi-user systems.  That had implications for
the ethos and the culture around it.  This may be new to you if you
started computing after, say, 1985.
When you log in, run "finger" to see
who's logged in and what they're doing.   You will be presented with
BB[fn:2] (bulletin-board) messages (system local messages that other people
post).  There was/is "talk" for connecting with other users on the
system.

One of the many things that was cool about the TOPS20 exec (shell) was the command
line help system. Type "?" at any point (no return) and you will see the list of
valid command options at that point. This was mimicked in Cisco IOS and Columbia
C-Kermit. Bits of it were re-implemented in [[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tcsh][tcsh
(TENEX C shell)]] and later in bash. Type "?". See what happens.

Triple bonus points - log in using telnet.  Who needs ssh?  Who needs
encrypted passwords on the wire?

** The people
*** RMS - sine non qua
I don't think I need to or can say anything new about Stallman, his
vision, his skill as a programmer,
his dedication, etc. For my entire career he was a force, a presence, a coding-monster,
a moral inspiration, etc. The Emacs story began 6 or 7 years before FSF, but I think
the sharing culture that created it was and is the heart of free software and where
it most clearly lives to this day. 

*** Guy Steele and the CMU connection 
Guy Steele contributed to the initial development of Emacs. He was a student at MIT
at the time. He later moved to Carnegie-Mellon University (CMU) as an
assistant professor.  He later co-authored one of the standard
references on C and helped standardize Java.
I'm assuming he brought Emacs with him from MIT. Secondary effects indicate that this is probably
true. I know of at least two other implementations of Emacs that happened at CMU
around that time: "[[https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gosling_Emacs][Gosmacs]]" by James
Gosling (later of Java fame), and [[https://github.com/PDP-10/fine][FINE (FINE Is
Not Emacs)]] for TOPS10 (more on that later). My future wife was an undergraduate
at CMU at the time and knew Steele and his family.

*** More CMU connections

The plot thickens. My older brother went to CMU as an undergrad in the fall of 1978.
He was running in the computing circles where Emacs versions were proliferating.
My first memory of Emacs is a stack of green-bar print-outs of the source code (TECO)
for Emacs that sat for a year or so in my younger brother's room. He and several of
his friends from CMU worked as interns during the summers for [[http://curious.galthub.com/blog/2022-03-24/][Steve
Wilhite]] (he of GIF file format fame) at CompuServe. Steve was developing compilers,
run-times and programming support tools (like editors). They brought FINE with them
from CMU. CompuServe ran (to its dying day) on DEC10s. I later worked for Steve in
my first job out of college. FINE was there and waiting for me.

*** The Columbian Exchange.

To keep this post mostly in the 70s I have to mention a group in passing that deserves
its own history.  It turns out I was in the right place at the right
time with (in retrospect) an incredible group of friends.

In high school, before the Apple-II, before Star Wars we had a
clique that called itself the "Computer Wizards". In many cases we were sons of professors
and the odd Bell Labs/Western electric/Battelle engineer. We made our own light sabers before
there was Star Wars merch on the shelves. Members of the group went on to places
like CMU, Stanford, MIT, Berkeley, Microsoft, Amazon, and
Google. Various of us were responsible
for things such as the Document Object Model (DOM), the Java VM at Sun, C++ standards,
bits of the Mach kernel (running to this day as the core OS of macs), high speed
networking research, tcsh, IETF standards work and managing a James Bond movie shooting
site in Puerto Rico that sometimes doubled as a world famous radio telescope. Several
migrated to CMU. Several more of us spent time working at Ohio State University Computer
and Information Science (osu-cis) in the early days of GNU/Gnu Emacs. For about 10
years there was a constant flow of people between OSU, CMU and, later, silicon valley.
Emacs was part of the ethos and the environment.
